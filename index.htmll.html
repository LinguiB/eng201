One body paragraph = 7 parts (plus an optional 3rd sub-idea and its evidence). Follow the order. Use at least two different sources. Each body paragraph develops one focal point from your thesis statement (Body 1 = focal point 1, and so on).

3 or more authors: APA 7 uses et al. from the first citation. In the reference list, you write all the authors’ names (up to 20).
⚠️ Don’t depend on direct quotes. Paraphrase or summarize most of the time. Quote only when the exact words matter (a definition, a powerful statement). A quote under 40 words goes in “quotation marks” with a page number; 40 words or more is a block quote. Try to have zero or one short quote per paragraph.
Reference list rules
New page titled References · alphabetical by first author’s surname · hanging indent · double-spaced.

Capital letters: the title of an article, webpage, video, or book = sentence case. Italics: the book, video, film, or webpage title, or the journal name + volume.

Paraphrase vs. summary

Paraphrase = one idea in your own words and your own sentence structure, about the same length as the original.
Summary = only the main point of a longer text, much shorter than the original.
Both need a citation. Both are better than a direct quote.

5 paraphrasing techniques

1. Change the words: use synonyms (keep technical terms).
2. Change the word class: “an increase in use” → “use increases”.
3. Change the structure: active ↔ passive; move the clauses.
4. Split or combine sentences.
5. Check the meaning against the original, then add a reporting verb and the citation.

4 steps to summarize

1. Read the text and find the main idea. 2. Note the 2–3 key supporting points. 3. Write from memory, in about one third of the length. 4. Cite the source.

Example (made-up source)

Original (Lee, 2021): “Teenagers who exchange messages with their friends on a daily basis report significantly higher levels of emotional closeness than teenagers who contact friends only occasionally.”

❌ Weak paraphrase (only a few words changed): Lee (2021) finds that teenagers who send messages to friends every day report much higher levels of emotional closeness than teenagers who contact friends only sometimes.

✅ Good paraphrase: Lee (2021) finds that daily messaging makes teens feel much closer to their friends than rare contact does.

✅ Summary: Lee (2021) shows that regular contact strengthens teens’ friendships.
